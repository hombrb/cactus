# Cactus Card Game Rules: The Complete Guide to Golf's Hidden Cousin

Cactus is a card game where you don't know your own cards. You peek at two of them once, memorize what you can, and spend the rest of the game trying to get rid of the bad ones before someone calls "Cactus" and forces a showdown. The lowest score wins, and if the caller guessed wrong, their score doubles.

If you searched for "cactus card game rules" and landed on pages about Blue Orange's party game or something called "Ouch!" or "What's the Point?", you were looking at the wrong game. This article covers the traditional Cactus card game played with a plain 52-card deck, no special components needed.


## What is the Cactus card game?

Cactus is a folk card game with no publisher, no box, and no official rulebook. It belongs to the [Golf family of card games](https://www.pagat.com/draw/golf.html) documented by Pagat.com, alongside better-known variants like [Cabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabo_(game)) and [Cambio](https://cambiocardgame.com/).

The game goes by a dozen names depending on where you learned it. In France it's called Dutch or Cactus. In Quebec, Tamalou. In Belgium and internationally, Cabo. In Latin America, Cambio or Cumbia. In Scandinavia, Pablo. You might also hear Kabo, Gabo, Marmotte, Dacz, or Salopette. They're all the same core game with minor rule differences from table to table. The [naming article](./cabo-cambio-golf-cactus-same-game.md) covers the full family tree.

The one English blog that correctly identifies the folk game is [Denexa Games' Cactus Golf write-up](https://www.denexa.com/blog/cactus-golf/). [BoardGameGeek](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/73664/cabo) lists the commercial Cabo version, which adapted these folk rules into a boxed product.


## Equipment and setup

You need a standard 52-card deck with no jokers. If you have 5 or more players, shuffle two decks together.

Deal 4 cards face down to each player, arranged in a 2x2 grid. This layout is fixed for the whole round. You never rearrange your cards or move them around. The grid matters because you need to remember which card is where.

Put the remaining deck in the center as the stock. Flip the top card face up next to it to start the discard pile.

Before the first turn, each player secretly peeks at their two nearest cards (the bottom row of their grid), then puts them back face down. That's all the information you start with.


## Card values

| Card | Value |
|------|-------|
| Red King (hearts, diamonds) | 0 |
| Ace | 1 |
| 2 through 10 | Face value |
| Jack | 11 |
| Queen | 12 |
| Black King (spades, clubs) | 13 |

The red/black King split is the signature rule that separates Cactus from simpler Golf variants. The red King is the best card in the game (0 points). The black King is the worst (13 points) but carries the strongest power. Holding an unknown King is a high-variance bet: it's either saving you or sinking you, and you might not find out until the final reveal.


## Turn structure

Play goes clockwise. On your turn, you do exactly one of two things.

**Draw from the stock.** Take the top card of the stock and look at it privately. Then choose:

- Swap it into one of your grid slots. The card it replaces goes face up onto the discard pile. No power activates.
- Discard it directly, face up. If the card has a power, the power triggers now.

**Take from the discard pile.** Take the face-up top card of the discard (you already know what it is). You must swap it into your grid. The replaced card goes to the discard pile. No power activates.

The critical asymmetry here: a power only fires when you draw a card from the stock and discard it without keeping it. Using a card for its value and using it for its power are mutually exclusive. This is the rule every source agrees on, and it's what keeps the game balanced.


## Powers

| Card | Power |
|------|-------|
| 7 or 8 | Peek at one of your own cards |
| 9 or 10 | Peek at one opponent's card |
| Jack or Queen | Blind swap: exchange one of your cards with one of theirs, neither card is revealed |
| Black King (spades, clubs) | Look at one of yours AND one of theirs, then choose whether to swap them |
| Ace (off by default) | Give the drawn card to an opponent, adding it to their layout |

The black King is both the worst card to hold and the best power to use. That's intentional: it's a "use it or suffer" card. If you draw one from the stock, you have a tough choice between an incredibly strong power and being stuck with 13 points.

The Jack and Queen swaps are blind. You're trading based on what you think you know, not what you can see. A player who's been paying attention can steal a card they watched an opponent place.

The Ace power (documented on the [Cabo Wikipedia page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabo_(game))) adds an extra card to someone's layout. It's brutal in short games, which is why most tables leave it turned off.


## Quick discard (snap)

At any moment, during anyone's turn or between turns, any player can slam a card from their layout onto the discard pile if its rank matches the top card of the discard.

**Correct guess:** The card is gone for good. That grid slot stays empty for the rest of the round. Your total score drops and you have fewer cards to worry about. This is the only way to shrink your layout below 4 cards.

**Wrong guess:** The card goes back face down to its slot, and you receive a penalty card face down in a new slot. A failed snap hurts twice: you've shown everyone what that card is, and now you have an extra unknown card.

The snap mechanic is what makes Cactus more than a memory exercise. It rewards constant attention to the discard pile and creates sudden bursts of tension when two players race to match the same card.


## Calling Cactus and scoring

After completing your turn (not instead of it), you can announce "Cactus" if you believe you have the lowest total score at the table.

Every other player then takes one final turn, in normal order. The caller does not play again.

After the final round, everyone flips their cards and adds up their scores.

**If the caller has strictly the lowest score:** they score 0 for the round. Everyone else scores their total.

**If the caller is not strictly the lowest (a tie counts as failure):** the caller's total is doubled. Everyone else scores their total normally.

Why does a tie count as failure? Because without that rule, calling would be risk-free whenever you could force a draw. The call has to be a bet, not a freebie.

The match continues across multiple rounds. When any player's cumulative score reaches 100, the match ends. The player with the lowest cumulative total wins.


## Key variants

There are no official rules, so every table plays a little differently. Here are the most common points of disagreement.

| Rule | Standard default | Common variants |
|------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Red King value | 0 | -1, -2 |
| Black King value | 13 | 15, 20, or 0 (school version) |
| Jack/Queen value | 11/12 | 10/10 (school version) |
| Cards peeked at start | 2 (nearest row) | 1, 3, or 2 of your choice |
| Power cards | 7/8, 9/10, J/Q, black K | Only the 8 (school version); 6/7, 8/9, 10/J, black K ([Cambio](https://cambiocardgame.com/) shift) |
| Ace power | Off | Give drawn card to opponent |
| Snap | On | Off (too chaotic for some tables) |
| Failed snap penalty | +1 card | +2 cards, or no penalty |
| Call threshold | None (call whenever) | Must believe you have 5 or less |
| Tie = failure | Yes | No (school version) |
| Caller success score | 0 | Actual total |
| Caller failure penalty | Score doubled | Score + 10, or score + 20 |
| Match limit | 100 cumulative points | 200 points, or fixed number of rounds |

The "school" version (what most French players learn as "Dutch") simplifies everything: all Kings worth 0, Jack/Queen worth 10, only the 8 has a power, call threshold of 5, ties are fine. It's faster and friendlier, but loses the strategic tension of the red/black King split.

The [Cambio](https://cambiocardgame.com/) variant shifts which cards carry powers down by one rank (6/7 peek self, 8/9 peek opponent, 10/Jack blind swap, black King informed swap), which changes hand evaluation significantly.


## Also known as

This game travels under many names: Dutch (France), Tamalou (Quebec), Cabo (Belgium, international), Cambio and Cumbia (Latin America), Pablo (Scandinavia), Kabo, Gabo, Marmotte, Dacz, Salopette, and of course Cactus. The rules shift slightly from name to name, but the skeleton is always the same: hidden cards, memory, powers, and a risky final call. For the full story of how one game ended up with so many names, see the [naming article](./cabo-cambio-golf-cactus-same-game.md).
