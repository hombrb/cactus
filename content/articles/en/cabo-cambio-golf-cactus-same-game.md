# Cabo vs. Cambio vs. Golf vs. Cactus: Same Game, Different Names

Cabo, Cambio, Golf, Cactus, Dutch, Tamalou, Pablo: these are all names for the same card game. You deal four hidden cards, peek at two, and spend the round trying to swap out bad cards and memorize good ones until someone calls the end. The lowest score wins. No publisher controls the rules, so every table and every country tweaked them and renamed the game along the way.

If you found this page searching for "Cabo card game" or "Cambio card game," you're probably wondering whether they're the same thing. Short answer: yes, with minor rule variations. Here's the full picture.


## One game, a dozen passports

This game has no official origin story. No designer stepped forward to claim it. It spread the way folk games always spread: someone taught it to someone else, who taught it to someone else, who changed a rule and gave it a new name.

The blog [No Point Tales](http://nopointtales.blogspot.com/2017/09/the-game-of-cabo-cambio-tamul-kabo-cabu.html) documented names collected while traveling through hostels and backpacker bars: Cabo, Cambio, Tamul, Kabo, Cabu, Kabul, Hampi, Badouk, Babadouk. Travel blogger [Yentl Doggen](https://yentldoggen.com/how-to-play-cabo-the-famous-travellers-card-game/) calls it "the famous traveler's card game," and that label fits. The game hitched rides across continents in the backpacks of twenty-somethings who learned it at a hostel in Thailand or a bar in Colombia and brought it home under whatever name they first heard.

In France, most people call it Cactus or Dutch. In Quebec, Tamalou. In Belgium and the international backpacker circuit, Cabo or Kabo. In Latin America, Cambio or Cumbia. In Scandinavia, Pablo. The French [Wikipedia article on Dutch](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_(jeu_de_cartes)) lists even more regional French names: Marmotte, Salopette, Gabo, Kobo. In Poland, the game is called Dacz (the name might come from "Dutch" or the Russian "dacha," but no one knows for sure).

[Pagat.com](https://www.pagat.com/draw/golf.html), the most thorough card game reference on the internet, files all of these under the Golf family. That's the academic umbrella term, though almost nobody at a real table calls it "Golf."


## The commercial Cabo vs. the traditional game

Here's a distinction that trips people up: there are two things called "Cabo."

The first is a **boxed card game** published by Bezier Games in 2010, created by Melissa Limes and Mandy Henning. They were inspired by [Golf](https://www.pagat.com/draw/golf.html) and Rat-a-Tat Cat (a 1995 Mensa Select winner). The commercial Cabo has a custom deck, standardized rules, and a published rulebook. It got a [second edition](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/271321/cabo-second-edition), and in February 2025 a third edition came out through Shareful Games LLC. You can find it on [BoardGameGeek](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/73664/cabo) and the [official Cabo site](https://www.cabogame.com/how-to-play).

The second is the **traditional folk game** that people have been playing with a standard 52-card deck for decades. No publisher, no box, no fixed rules. This is the game that travels under all those names listed above.

Both are legitimate. They share the same DNA: hidden cards in a grid, peeking, swapping, memory, and a risky endgame call. The commercial version standardized one particular rule set and put it in a box. The folk version kept mutating. If someone says "let's play Cabo" at a hostel, they almost certainly mean the folk game with a regular deck. If someone pulls out a purple box, that's the Bezier Games product.


## Name by name: what changes

The names aren't purely cosmetic. Different regions tend to play with different rule clusters.

**Cabo / Kabo / Kabou** is the name used across Belgium and the international backpacker scene. Players usually follow the standard ruleset: red Kings worth 0, black Kings worth 13, four tiers of power cards (7/8 peek your own, 9/10 peek an opponent's, J/Q blind swap, black King look-and-swap), and a snap mechanic.

**Cambio / Cumbia** dominates in Spanish-speaking countries and Latin America. [Pagat.com](https://www.pagat.com/draw/golf.html) notes that "cambio" means "exchange" in Spanish, and some researchers think this might actually be the original name. The [commercial Cambio version](https://cambiocardgame.com/) uses shifted power ranks: 6/7 peek your own, 8/9 peek an opponent's, 10/J blind swap, black King informed swap. That one-rank shift changes hand evaluation in subtle ways.

**Cactus** is the most widespread name in France. The rules are typically the standard set described above. The name probably comes from the spiky feeling of getting stuck with a bad hand, but honestly, nobody knows.

**Dutch** is used in southwestern France and is sometimes called "Dutch Toulousain." This usually refers to the simplified school variant: all Kings worth 0, face cards worth 10, only the 8 has a power (peek at your own card), and you need to believe your score is 5 or below to call. It's the version most French kids learn first.

**Tamalou** is the Quebec name and pops up in some French regions too.

**Pablo** shows up in Nordic countries.

**Marmotte, Salopette, Gabo, Kobo** are regional French names documented on the French Wikipedia article for [Dutch](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_(jeu_de_cartes)).

**Golf** is the family name used by [Pagat.com](https://www.pagat.com/draw/golf.html). It covers the entire category, including simpler variants (4-card Golf, 6-card Golf) that don't have power cards at all.

**Dacz** is the Polish variant. The name might derive from "Dutch" or from Russian "dacha." Polish players tend to have their own spin on the power assignments.


## The three rule families

Tracking every individual variant is a losing game. But if you zoom out, the dozens of house rules cluster into three main families.

**School / Simple.** All Kings are worth 0. Jack and Queen are worth 10. Only the 8 has a power (peek at one of your own cards). You can only call when you believe your total is 5 or below. Ties don't count as failure for the caller. This is "Dutch" as most French schoolyards play it. It's fast, forgiving, and a good entry point. The tradeoff: without the red/black King split and the full power system, the strategic ceiling is much lower.

**Standard.** Red King 0, black King 13. Four power tiers: 7/8 peek your own, 9/10 peek an opponent's, J/Q blind swap, black King look-and-swap. Snap enabled (match the discard pile at any time to dump a card, but get penalized for guessing wrong). Caller must have strictly the lowest score or their total doubles. Match runs to 100 cumulative points. This is what most organized play follows and what the French Wikipedia article technically describes under "Dutch." It's also the ruleset used in the [Cactus card game rules](./cactus-card-game-rules.md) article.

**Commercial Cabo.** Custom deck with its own rank assignments (6/7 peek, 8/9 spy, 10/J swap, black K informed swap). Published rulebook with specific scoring rules. Easier to pick up because the rules aren't up for debate, but you need to buy the game. Details on the [official site](https://www.cabogame.com/how-to-play) and [BoardGameGeek](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/73664/cabo).

Most arguments about rules come from people who learned different families and assume theirs is "the right one." None of them is. They're all branches of the same tree.


## How to play any version for free

You don't need to buy a commercial version or track down a specific deck. A standard 52-card deck is all it takes, and you probably have one in a drawer somewhere.

If you want to play on your phone, the [Cactus app](/) is a free PWA that runs in your browser. No download, no account, no ads. It uses a standard deck and lets you configure the rules: pick the school preset, the standard preset, or assign powers to whatever cards you want.

The app is in French, so here's a quick translation cheat sheet: "Jouer sur ce telephone" means "Play on this phone," "Regles" means "Rules," "Reglages" means "Settings." It works offline and on any device.

Cactus also makes a surprisingly good [two-player game](./best-card-games-for-two-players.md), since the memory pressure and snap timing stay intense even with just two people at the table.

Whatever you call it and however you play it, it's the same game at its core: hide your cards, remember what you can, swap out the junk, and call the end before someone else beats you to it. [Give it a try.](/)
